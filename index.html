Want to Know If the Bottom Is In? Read this.

Since $QQQ just printed a gap-up candle, the next thing I'll be watching is the Follow-Through Day (FTD).

One of William O'Neil's most important rules for confirming a market bottom.

How it works:
- Market is in a correction. The first day an index closes higher = Day 1 of a rally attempt.
- Days 2-3 don't need to be up. The rally stays alive as long as Day 1's low holds.
- Then on Day 4-7, watch for one major index (S&P 500 or Nasdaq) to gain 1.25%+ on volume higher than the day before.

That's the follow-through day. Downtrend over. Confirmed uptrend.

The rules that matter:
- Not every FTD works - many fail
- But no bull market has EVER started without one.
- Undercut Day 1's low -> the count resets
- FTDs after Day 10 carry lower success rates

What to actually do on an FTD:
Don't go all in. Buy starter positions in leading stocks breaking out of sound bases. If the rally is real, they work - and you add. If it fails, you're barely exposed.

O'Neil's whole point: you don't predict bottoms. You confirm them.

Corrections are for building the watchlist. The FTD tells you when to use it.

Thoughts on $AMZN and AWS

By now the cat is out of the bag. Andy Jassy believes AWS can eventually become a $1 trillion revenue business. Whether it gets there or not is almost beside the point. The more interesting question is what a business like that could actually be worth. Let’s do some simple math.

If AWS reaches $1 trillion in annual revenue and earns a 35% operating margin, it would produce $350 billion of operating income. After taxes, that’s approximately $280 billion of annual net income. That’s about $767 million every day, or roughly $32 million every hour, every hour of every day. Now ask yourself a simple question.

What is a business worth that earns $280 billion every year? At 20x earnings, AWS alone would be worth about $5.6 trillion. At 25×, about $7 trillion.

The part that fascinates me isn’t the valuation. It’s the capital allocation. Amazon expects to spend roughly $220 billion this year, and Jassy already said spending will increase again because demand still exceeds supply.

Eventually, every great infrastructure business reaches a point where it stops primarily building capacity and starts harvesting the returns. CapEx doesn’t disappear, but it doesn’t need to. If spending eventually grows slower than earnings, the economics could become astonishing.

This is where I think many investors make a mistake. They spend all their time debating next quarter while management is building the next decade. Great businesses often look expensive because investors measure today’s earnings instead of tomorrow’s economics.

Whether AWS becomes a $1 trillion business isn’t really the lesson. The lesson is that extraordinary businesses can become so large that our brains almost reject the possibility. The biggest investing mistakes are often not overestimating great businesses. They’re underestimating just how extraordinary they can eventually become.

Andrew Ho@andrewho0321h
I'm actually fairly bearish on frontier lab valuations. I've never seen the reasons articulated to my satisfaction, so before I go to sleep, I wanted to quickly jot down my thinking here.

The basic issue is that the labs are highly unprofitable. This may seem like a simple point, but private market valuations can be relatively irrational; however, like with $SPCX, post-IPO pricing will likely be much more punishing, especially as the standard 6-month lockup period expires and selling pressure intensifies.

Many people claim that the labs have high margins. Yet even with high margins, a valuation of $1T would be justified only if the labs were doing nothing aside from serving inference (thus reducing costs only to those relevant to inference) and posting annual revenue numbers in the $100-200 billion range assuming ~80% gross margin and a 20x earnings multiple.

This assumption is obviously not true, because the frontier labs have to continually spend money training the next generation of models. This is because of market competition from runner-up firms. For example, if OpenAI had paused model development last year, there would no longer be any point in paying GPT-5 API prices when you can just use Qwen or Kimi instead for much cheaper. Thus, the labs are forced to invest ever-increasing amounts of money in model training, in a way such that at any given point of time, the amount you're forced to invest in the next model is dramatically higher than the amount of money you're actually making, because even if your revenue goes up with higher model capabilities, so do your future training costs. This is a profoundly punishing dynamic which severely penalizes frontrunners.

(There is also a related subpoint where frontier labs claim they can distill their leading models to win out at lower intelligence levels as well. This makes no sense because the revenue numbers involved are far too low when taking into consideration the rather low margin of such inference.)

Frontier lab valuations appear largely to be based on the assumption that as you scale up, the capabilities which emerge will be sufficiently general and profound that we'll see explosive growth (https://t.co/RqmkltVpM3) from things akin to AI agents starting and autonomously managing entire companies of subagents. But it's not clear to me that this is the case; indeed, as I mentioned in my previous post (https://t.co/3URAcJ4XkJ), I believe that capabilities growth will be slower, spikier, and more data-limited than people currently assume. It may be the case that eventually we will see explosive growth of this nature with full automation of the economy, but at the very least my viewpoint implies much longer (multi-decade) timelines until we reach this point. It is not clear to me that the frontier labs will be able to operate unprofitably for so long, although I suppose maybe this foreshadows some sort of inevitable nationalization.

I also want to make a broader point about technological diffusion. The reason why technological diffusion is slow isn't just because, e.g., old people take a long time to learn how to use technology (although this is of course a contributing factor to some degree). In my view, it's because when a new, revolutionary technology comes along, the ways to incorporate that technology into subsequent developments are not always obvious, and in fact they cannot necessarily be arrived at through the application of pure reason. If they could be, then perhaps frontier models, at a certain point, would have a perfect understanding of how the LLM application layer should be developed, and they would then autonomously code, deploy, and sell such a layer.

But it seems more plausible to me that this diffusion is limited moreso by the hard problem of economic calculation--that is to say, the Hayekian notion through which the price system gradually promotes efficient allocation of resources and which cannot be simulated through central planning--and that even if we froze current capability levels at today's levels, it would take well over two decades to fully integrate in LLMs into our lives. Such a view is consequently rather bearish for the continued profitability of labs as it reduces their prospects for finding, say, something else comparable in profitability to coding agents, which seems to have been a somewhat lucky discovery by Anthropic to begin with. That is to say, even if you spam FDEs you aren't necessarily going to be able to just figure out the "correct" product shapes fast enough.

Overall, I don't think that people have clearly reasoned through their mental models for why lab equity should be worth as much as it currently is, and that if you actually bother to write down such a model, you may not arrive at the conclusion that you want to arrive at. This isn't to say that I don't expect AI to experience a huge (industry-wide) boom in the coming decades, but just that I'm not entirely sure I would buy OpenAI or Anthropic stock at latest valuations if I were given the opportunity to do so.

Of course, as an ex-lab employee, arguably this is talking against my own book; I should really be giving people more reasons to be bullish. But in the end, my influence is so small that it doesn't make a difference, so why not have some fun?

美联储最终将联邦基金利率维持在3.50%—3.75%，但这并不是一次真正意义上的鸽派暂停。
本次决议以9票赞成、3票反对通过，三名反对者希望直接加息25个基点。
声明一边承认美国经济仍在稳健扩张、生产率和资本投资较强，另一边继续强调通胀高于2%的目标，能源等供给冲击仍在推高部分价格。

对于科技股来说，这件事非常重要。
很多AI公司的估值，依赖的是未来几年甚至十几年后的利润。只要资金成本继续维持高位，市场就会用更高的折现率重新计算这些利润今天值多少钱。
所以昨晚能抗住的，是那些已经证明，自己的AI投入能够跑赢资金成本的公司。

微软的成绩单很亮眼
微软季度营收达到900亿，同比增长18%；Microsoft Cloud收入达到593亿，同比增长27%；Azure收入增长43%，全年Azure收入也首次超过1000亿。
与此同时，微软单季度资本开支已经达到410亿，但经营现金流仍有554亿，自由现金流为196亿。
跟之前暴跌的几家巨头完全不一样，微软的账上还躺着这么多的自由现金流和经营现金流。
这组数据说明，市场并不是单纯反对巨额资本开支。

微软真正证明的是：AI基建不是只在花钱，它已经开始形成收入、订单和现金流的闭环。

Meta的问题不是AI没有作用。
它的推荐算法、广告转化率和用户活跃度都在受益于AI。
资本担心 AI 创造出来的增量收入，目前还没有完全追上数据中心、算力、人才和模型研发带来的成本增长。

所以微软和Meta放在一起看，市场的新标准已经很清楚了：必须证明收入增速能够覆盖资本开支，利润率没有被持续吞噬，自由现金流还能支撑下一轮投资。

FOMC没有加息，我们却开始重估AI的回报率

前两天，我给存储板块留了几个预测点位： $MU 看700—750， $SNDK 看 950 附近，SK海力士看 900 附近。

今天晚上目前
mu 856 闪迪1240 海力士1041
恭喜跟上的大家

不过比点位本身更重要的是：为什么好的财报，加上没有加息的FOMC，还是没能让市场彻底放松下来？

因为这一轮超级财报周，市场正在同时重估两种回报率
一种是无风险利率
另一种是AI资本开支的回报率

先看FOMC。
美联储最终将联邦基金利率维持在3.50%—3.75%，但这并不是一次真正意义上的鸽派暂停。
本次决议以9票赞成、3票反对通过，三名反对者希望直接加息25个基点。
声明一边承认美国经济仍在稳健扩张、生产率和资本投资较强，另一边继续强调通胀高于2%的目标，能源等供给冲击仍在推高部分价格。
换句话说，美联储没有加息，不代表加息风险已经消失。
它只是决定继续等数据。
而今晚公布的二季度GDP初值、6月PCE以及初请失业金，就会直接决定市场如何理解这次暂停：究竟是通胀正在降温，还是美联储只是在暂时观望。

对于科技股来说，这件事非常重要。
很多AI公司的估值，依赖的是未来几年甚至十几年后的利润。只要资金成本继续维持高位，市场就会用更高的折现率重新计算这些利润今天值多少钱。
所以昨晚能抗住的，是那些已经证明，自己的AI投入能够跑赢资金成本的公司。

微软的成绩单很亮眼
微软季度营收达到900亿，同比增长18%；Microsoft Cloud收入达到593亿，同比增长27%；Azure收入增长43%，全年Azure收入也首次超过1000亿。
与此同时，微软单季度资本开支已经达到410亿，但经营现金流仍有554亿，自由现金流为196亿。
跟之前暴跌的几家巨头完全不一样，微软的账上还躺着这么多的自由现金流和经营现金流。
这组数据说明，市场并不是单纯反对巨额资本开支。
只要云收入、订单和现金流能够同步增长，资本开支再大，也可以被理解成扩张。
微软真正证明的是：AI基建不是只在花钱，它已经开始形成收入、订单和现金流的闭环。

Meta的成绩就没那么好了
Meta本季度营收达到608亿，同比增长28%；广告展示量增长14%，单价增长12%，说明它的核心广告业务并没有失速。
但同期总成本增长55%，营业利润反而下降8%，营业利润率从43%降到31%；单季度资本开支达到310.8亿，自由现金流却只剩7.84亿。
Meta的问题不是AI没有作用。
它的推荐算法、广告转化率和用户活跃度都在受益于AI。
资本担心 AI 创造出来的增量收入，目前还没有完全追上数据中心、算力、人才和模型研发带来的成本增长。

所以微软和Meta放在一起看，市场的新标准已经很清楚了：必须证明收入增速能够覆盖资本开支，利润率没有被持续吞噬，自由现金流还能支撑下一轮投资。

存储面对的其实也是同一道题
SK海力士本季度营收达到79.3万亿韩元，营业利润达到60.5万亿韩元，营业利润率高达76%，营业利润同比增长557%，全部创下历史纪录。
但海力士股价在财报后依然一度下跌接近10%。
原因不是HBM突然没有需求，而是业绩没有超过已经被抬得极高的市场预期。
市场开始追问的

HBM的高价格还能维持多久？
产能持续扩张以后，会不会重新出现供给压力？
当前股价里，又已经提前计入了多少未来利润？

这也是为什么MU跌到706、海力士跌破900，并不能简单理解成产业逻辑消失了，因为人家存储和AI主线没结束，人家的内存也没有因为股价下跌而卖的更便宜。

准确地说，是三个因素叠加：宏观层面的资金成本仍然偏高；财报预期已经提前打满；高杠杆资金在同一时间集中降低风险。

产业没有突然反转，但估值和仓位开始重新定价。

今晚苹果和亚马逊的财报，会继续验证这套逻辑
苹果除了看iPhone卖了多少，而是服务业务能否继续增长、组件成本上涨会不会侵蚀毛利率，以及Apple Intelligence什么时候能够真正转化成收入。

亚马逊则要看AWS。
如果AWS增速和利润率继续改善，就说明微软不是个例，全球云厂商的AI投入依然能够被实际需求消化；如果AWS增速放缓、资本开支却继续上升，市场对整个AI基建链条的回报率都会更加挑剔。

不过要注意一个很多像我一样的新手容易误解的地方

股价高 ≠ 公司价值更高

因为每家公司发行的股票数量不同，所以真正比较公司大小，要看总市值（Market Cap）。

举个例子：

A 公司：100 美元/股，发行 10 亿股 = 市值 1000 亿美元
B 公司：50 美元/股，发行 30 亿股 = 市值 1500 亿美元

虽然 B 的股价更低，但公司整体反而更大。

就目前来看：

可口可乐（KO）总市值约 3600 亿美元左右
百事（PEP）总市值约 1900 亿美元左右

也就是说：

股价：PEP > KO
公司市值：KO > PEP

按照投资风格来分的话
英伟达（NVDA） 特斯拉（TSLA）属于成长股（Growth Stocks）

成长股卖的不是现在，而是未来。
投资者买它，不是因为它今天赚多少钱。
而是相信它未来几年甚至十几年，还会高速增长
所以市场愿意给它更高的估值

它的特点是
营收增长快
用户增长快
利润可能不高，甚至暂时亏损
公司会把赚来的钱继续投入研发 扩张 而不是大量分红。
所以成长股的股价波动通常比消费股更大

可口可乐（KO） 百事（PEP）属于消费必需品 + 防御型 + 蓝筹股（Blue Chip）

为什么叫防御型股票？
因为它们卖的是生活必需消费品。
无论经济怎么样，人们都会继续消费
现在也被大家称为老登股😹

所以相比科技股，它们的收入和现金流通常更加稳定。
这类股票的特点就是
业绩稳定
现金流充足
分红稳定（很多都是股息贵族）
波动相对较小
牛市可能跑不过科技股，但熊市通常更抗跌

投资者买它，不是因为它今天赚多少钱。
而是相信它未来几年甚至十几年，还会高速增长
所以市场愿意给它更高的估值

它的特点是
营收增长快
用户增长快
利润可能不高，甚至暂时亏损
公司会把赚来的钱继续投入研发 扩张 而不是大量分红。
所以成长股的股价波动通常比消费股更大

可口可乐（KO） 百事（PEP）属于消费必需品 + 防御型 + 蓝筹股（Blue Chip）

为什么叫防御型股票？
因为它们卖的是生活必需消费品。
无论经济怎么样，人们都会继续消费
现在也被大家称为老登股😹

所以相比科技股，它们的收入和现金流通常更加稳定。
这类股票的特点就是
业绩稳定
现金流充足
分红稳定（很多都是股息贵族）
波动相对较小
牛市可能跑不过科技股，但熊市通常更抗跌

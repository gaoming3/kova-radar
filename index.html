This seems like a good time to explain something most retail investors have probably never heard of: a stock collar.

Full disclosure: I used AI to help me explain this because I’m not smart enough to teach options without turning it into complete gibberish.

But I have personally used collars many times on large stock positions when I still believed in the company but wanted to feel safer holding it through complete chaos.

Here’s a simplified example:

Let’s say you own 1,000 shares of $NVDA at $160, making your position worth $160,000.

You believe in NVIDIA long term, so you don’t want to panic-sell. But you also don’t want to wake up tomorrow and discover another piece of news just erased $20,000 from your account.

You could:

Buy 10 January $140 puts.

Each option controls 100 shares, so 10 puts protect all 1,000 shares.

Those puts give you the right to sell your shares for $140, even if NVDA crashes to $100, $50 or zero.

That’s the insurance.

Insurance costs money, so to help pay for it, you then:

Sell 10 January $200 covered calls.

Someone pays you for the right to buy your shares at $200.

That’s the collar.

Let’s pretend the puts cost $10 and the calls pay you $5. Your net cost would be $5 per share, or $5,000 total.

At expiration, your effective floor would therefore be roughly $135, not $140, because you have to include the $5 insurance cost.

Your effective ceiling would be roughly $195, because your shares can be called away at $200 and you spent $5 creating the collar.

So what happens?

Below $140, the puts protect you from additional losses.

Between $140 and $200, you continue participating in the stock’s movement.

Above $200, your shares will likely be called away for $200.

The protection disappears when the options expire.

There is no free lunch here.

The risks:

If NVDA stays flat, you could lose the money spent on the hedge.

If NVDA explodes higher, you sacrificed everything above the call strike.

Your shares can potentially be called away early.

Wide option spreads can make entering and exiting expensive.

The collar only protects you until its expiration date.

The tax treatment can be complicated.

Closing or rolling the position early may cost money.

If you want to keep every share, you need to manage the position before expiration instead of ignoring it.

Why would anyone intentionally cap their upside?

Because sometimes protecting a large amount of capital is more important than squeezing every possible dollar out of a trade.

I’ve used collars when:

I had too much money concentrated in one stock.

I still believed in the long-term thesis.

Selling immediately could create a large tax bill.

I was using margin and couldn’t afford another massive drop.

I wanted protection through earnings or some major event.

I was willing to sacrifice some upside to sleep better at night.

You can also collar only part of a position.

I recently protected half of one of my own large positions. Half received a downside floor, while the other half kept unlimited upside.

A collar isn’t a way to make free money. It’s insurance.

If the stock never crashes, the insurance can feel like wasted money. If the stock gets obliterated, it can feel like the smartest money you ever spent.

Most people only learn how hedging works after they desperately need it.

Learn it before you do.

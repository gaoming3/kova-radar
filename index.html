They see "a gap, a candle, a guess."

A tiny group of vol desks sees a ledger of forced trades already written from the close to the open.

Dealers carry the other side of the options book. Net GEX, their total gamma (how fast an option's delta, its share-equivalent exposure, changes as price moves), is estimated from SPY open interest, the count of open contracts, at -1.01B. Negative gamma means they must sell weakness and buy strength to stay hedged, which stretches moves through levels. The average retail trader reads a fast drop as a bargain. A desk reads it as dealers forced to sell the next leg.

Same tape. Same print. Completely opposite conclusion.

The difference isn't the indicator. It isn't the entry. It's who is forced to hedge, and where.

Here's the ledger, level by level →

WHERE DEALERS ARE FORCED TO TRADE:

→ 7,332 (SPY 731.61). The flip line, where dealer gamma turns from negative to positive. Spot SPX equivalent of 7,219.7 sits 112.5 points below it. That's not resistance. That's the border of the regime that amplifies every move.

→ 7,243.4. The upper half-EM shelf, half the expected move (the one-day range options price) above the close. In negative gamma, strength here gets bought by dealers, not faded.

→ 7,118 (7,115.6 to 7,122.1). Three independent levels line up just under the lower shelf at 7,122.1. That's not a random support line. That's confluence.

→ 7,015 (SPY 700). The modeled put wall, the biggest put strike, 106.7 points below the lower shelf. Below 7,122 dealers sell into the air above that floor.

→ 7,767 (SPY 775). The modeled call wall, 523.6 points above the upper shelf. That's not a target. That's a strike too far to matter tomorrow.

WHAT THE THETA CLOCK CHARGES:

→ 9:30 AM. The at-the-money straddle, one call plus one put at the current price, costs 98.3 SPX points, about 9,832 dollars, only 0.81x the 121.3-point expected move.

Week ahead, 16–20 Nov

Most calendars this week list Nvidia on Tuesday and Anthropic on Thursday as two separate events.

They're the same trade.

Nvidia reports Q3 on Tuesday at 22:20 CET. It's the first report since Jensen Huang stepped down on 12 Nov, with CFO Colette Kress as interim CEO. The company guided $108B in revenue and consensus sits around $109B. The Q4 guide will move the stock more than the beat.

Two days later Anthropic starts trading on Nasdaq as $ANT. Range $70 to $78, up to $64.4B raised. That's more than double Saudi Aramco's record.

What I haven't seen anyone connect yet is who else is buying.

Seven of Anthropic's compute suppliers are taking $26B of stock at the IPO price. Google and Nvidia about $10B each, with AMD, Broadcom, Micron, Microsoft and TSMC splitting the rest. The filing says the proceeds go to general purposes, compute and infrastructure included.

So $NVDA reports on Tuesday, then writes its customer a check worth roughly 9% of a quarter's revenue, and part of that money comes back as chip orders.

The bull read is simple. Suppliers are buying at the same price as you, with real money.

The bear read is that some of next year's AI demand is being funded by the companies that will book it as revenue. And per the filing, the supplier shares skip the usual lockup.

The rest of the week

Tue 14:30 CET, US retail sales for October. Consensus +0.2% after +0.8%.

Wed 20:00 CET, FOMC minutes. Two members wanted a hike in October, and the minutes were written before the hot October CPI.

Wed and Thu, Target, Lowe's, TJX and then Walmart. By Thursday lunch we'll know how the US consumer is holding up going into the holidays.

I'll be up late on Tuesday for this one.

Is a supplier buying into its customer's IPO a sign of conviction, or a loop you'd rather not own?

Sunday Thoughts with Beav 🦫

Is trading options really worth it?

I’ve been trading options for years, and looking back, it’s been one hell of a roller coaster. Incredible highs, devastating lows, and countless moments where I thought I had finally figured it out, only to end up right back where I started.

And lately I’ve been asking myself, for all the time, stress, and money I’ve put into options, how much actual wealth have I built?

The reality is, probably not nearly as much as I could have taking a different approach.

Options are a difficult game to win consistently, especially when you’re buying short-dated contracts. You’re not just fighting the direction of the market, you’re fighting TIME.

You can research a company, predict the direction correctly, and STILL lose money because you weren’t right fast enough. Time decay, changes in volatility, and expiration dates are constantly working against you.

Being right doesn’t always mean making money.

Then there’s the psychological side. The constant monitoring, emotional highs and lows, oversizing, chasing losses, and pressure to catch the next big move.

At some point, you have to ask yourself, am I actually building wealth or just chasing the next dopamine hit?

I’m not saying options don’t have their place, but I’ve realized there’s a big difference between trading to make money and investing to build wealth.

Buying quality companies, investing consistently in ETFs, holding shares long term, and allowing compounding to do its job can be far less stressful and involve less risk than leveraged options trading.

You don’t have to make money fast to build wealth. Sometimes trying to accelerate the process is exactly what’s holding you back.

I’ve spent years learning how to trade options. Maybe the next chapter is learning when NOT to trade them.

Because the goal isn’t to beat the market every week. It’s to build wealth, protect your capital, and enjoy your life along the way.

Let’s have an amazing week✌🏼💜🦫

My thoughts on the current state of crypto/markets (and what's next):

• In general, the world is in a volatile/scary place right now. Cyberattacks, FAA flight chaos, etc. I think people are really starting to worry about where they can store capital (this thesis is backed by the general stock market dip)

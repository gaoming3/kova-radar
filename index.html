Melvin@MelvinInvests18h
If you own ANY AI stocks, you need to read this.

“Our momentum is real. Our demand is accelerating. Our supply is growing. Our customers and our partners are with us. And employees are committed.” That was how NVIDIA's interim CEO Colette Kress ended what might be one of the most important earnings calls in the company's history. NVIDIA came into this quarter facing some serious concerns. Jensen Huang's departure, circular financing, the sustainability of AI spending, declining margins, export restrictions, and whether the company could continue executing without its founder. And I think management did a pretty good job addressing most of them.

First, the numbers were absolutely incredible. NVIDIA reported $116 billion in quarterly revenue, more than doubling year over year and growing 20% sequentially. Data center revenue reached $108 billion, up 21% quarter over quarter, while Vera Rubin generated nearly $20 billion in its first quarter of production shipments, making it the fastest product ramp in NVIDIA's history. Management also guided for $130 billion in Q4 revenue and raised its fiscal 2028 revenue growth outlook from 70% to 75%. Think about that for a second. We're talking about a company already generating more than $100 billion in quarterly revenue that still expects 75% annual growth in fiscal 2028. One of the biggest concerns was obviously Jensen Huang's departure. Interestingly, Jensen was only mentioned twice by name during the entire call. It almost felt like management wanted to distance the conversation from Jensen and shift investors' attention back to the business. The board explicitly stated that Jensen would not return as CEO and refused to discuss the ongoing investigation. Instead, Colette emphasized that NVIDIA's employees, leadership team, customers, and suppliers remain fully committed. She even mentioned that TSMC's leadership had been in NVIDIA's offices this week. The company also confirmed that Vera Rubin Ultra remains on track for next year, with Feynman expected in 2028. Now, despite the board saying Jensen isn't coming back, I personally still think there's a small chance we could eventually see him return in some capacity. I wouldn't bet on it, but I wouldn't completely rule it out either.

Another major concern was circular financing, particularly NVIDIA's financial relationships with neoclouds and other AI infrastructure companies. Investors have been worried that NVIDIA is essentially financing customers who then purchase its GPUs. Management clarified that many of its guarantees only take effect once customers have secured financing and their infrastructure is ready for deployment. If those conditions aren't met, certain commitments can be reduced or eliminated. Colette also confirmed this wasn't a new policy, just additional disclosure explaining how the arrangements work. That doesn't eliminate the risk, especially with NVIDIA carrying $118 billion in guarantees and $32 billion in neocloud related commitments, but it does provide more transparency. And speaking of neoclouds, I thought this was one of the most bullish parts of the entire call for companies like $NBIS and $CRWV. NVIDIA's AI Cloud, Industrial, and Enterprise segment generated $47 billion in revenue, growing 18% sequentially and nearly tripling year over year. Management emphasized that neoclouds are becoming increasingly important to the AI ecosystem, helping enterprises, governments, and AI labs access computing capacity.

NVIDIA revealed that Hopper GPUs are being recontracted at up to twice their original rental prices, while B300 rental rates have increased more than 60% since introduction. Management also confirmed that its compute remains fully utilized across every hyperscaler and neocloud it serves. That's a pretty big deal because one of the biggest bearish arguments against neoclouds has been that their expensive GPUs would rapidly lose value as newer hardware became available. Instead, we're seeing older GPUs retain significant economic value while newer hardware commands higher rental rates. $NBIS jumped more than 12% following those comments, and I think it's pretty clear why. If these pricing and utilization trends continue, the economics for well positioned neocloud operators could be considerably better than many investors expected. Another concern was whether the AI infrastructure boom was approaching its peak. NVIDIA pushed back with some pretty incredible numbers. Cloud industry backlog increased from $2 trillion to $2.5 trillion in just three months, while hyperscaler cloud revenue growth accelerated for the sixth consecutive quarter. Meanwhile, token volumes on OpenRouter increased 35 times year over year, with AI agents now accounting for approximately 80% of tokens. Token prices have also declined nearly 60% since May, encouraging significantly greater usage. Essentially, AI is becoming cheaper to use, but consumption is growing so quickly that demand for computing infrastructure continues to accelerate.

Margins were another major topic. NVIDIA expects Q4 gross margins to decline to 71%–72%, but management explicitly described that quarter as the bottom, with margins expected to recover to 72%–73% in fiscal 2028. Colette explained that NVIDIA had honored existing purchase orders despite rising supply costs and expects updated pricing to help stabilize margins. That's reassuring considering the concerns around memory costs, advanced packaging, and competition from custom AI chips. Management also addressed supply constraints, confirming that NVIDIA has secured additional memory and advanced packaging capacity. Vera Rubin is being deployed roughly twice as fast as Grace Blackwell, with hyperscalers bringing the platform into production in approximately one month. NVIDIA is also prioritizing customers that have secured financing, sufficient power, and data centers ready to deploy its hardware. That's particularly important because demand means very little if customers can't actually install and operate the infrastructure.

There were also questions about a potential slowdown in 2028 due to political uncertainty, electricity constraints, and opposition to data center construction. Management acknowledged those risks but emphasized that customers are still requesting more supply than NVIDIA can currently deliver. The company also established a National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee to strengthen engagement with the US government. Importantly, its guidance already excludes future licensed Hopper shipments to China, although the latest export restrictions are still being evaluated. Another encouraging development was capital allocation. NVIDIA returned $28 billion to shareholders during the quarter, including $22 billion in buybacks and $6 billion in dividends. It also increased its repurchase authorization by $150 billion, leaving $227 billion available at quarter-end. Management intends to execute that authorization by the end of fiscal 2028 while continuing to invest in supply chain expansion and selected infrastructure partnerships.

Overall, I think NVIDIA delivered exactly the kind of earnings call investors needed following the uncertainty of the past week. Management addressed nearly every major concern, raised its long-term growth outlook, and showed that demand for AI infrastructure remains exceptionally strong. Of course, Jensen's departure is still a major uncertainty, circular financing risks haven't completely disappeared, and customers will eventually need to demonstrate sufficient returns on their enormous AI investments. But when you have a company generating $116 billion in quarterly revenue, guiding for $130 billion next quarter, and still expecting 75% revenue growth in fiscal 2028, it's pretty difficult to argue that the underlying business is falling apart. Whether that's enough to reverse the recent stock decline remains to be seen, but I think NVIDIA gave the bulls plenty of ammunition tonight.
